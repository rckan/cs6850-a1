\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Fairness in Influence Blocking: From Cascades to Equitable Misinformation Mitigation}\\[2pt]
{\normalsize Rachel Kan, Matthew Zhang}
\end{center}
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\noindent We study four papers on choosing influential nodes to \emph{block} misinformation fairly: two that block a rumor with a competing truth campaign, one adding group fairness (FWRRS \cite{fwrrs}) and one adding user bias (CICMB \cite{cicmb}), and two on fairness objectives, a welfare approach for influence maximization \cite{welfare} and a demographic-parity approach for blocking \cite{fang}. All four build on the Independent Cascade (IC) model and the result that greedy seeding is within $1-1/e$ of optimal whenever expected spread is monotone and submodular \cite{kleinberg}.

\section{Main technical content}

\subsection{Fair truth campaigning: FWRRS \cite{fwrrs}}
Fake (negative) and true (positive) news spread under the campaign-oblivious IC model (COICM) with shared edge probabilities, and truth wins ties. Given rumor sources $S_N$, the \emph{blocking power} of truth seeds $S_P$ is the number of \emph{saved nodes}, $B(S_P)=s^-(S_N,\emptyset)-s^-(S_N,S_P)$. Fairness is \emph{maximin}: maximize $\min_c B_c(S_P)/s^-_c(S_N,\emptyset)$, the worst community's saved fraction, and then maximize total blocking among maximin solutions. FWRRS (Fairness-aware Weighted Reversible Reachable System) estimates infection probabilities by Monte Carlo, samples weighted reverse-reachable trees with D-SSA to estimate each node's per-community blocking power, and picks $k$ seeds by a six-step greedy rule that favors the worst-off community. On six real networks (Louvain communities) and five synthetic HICH-BA graphs, it beats Degree, CMIA-O, BIOG and TIB-Solver and two new fair baselines (Parity Seeding, Fair-CMIA-O), with up to ${\sim}10\%$ more saved nodes and ${\sim}57\%$ higher maximin. Fairness-blind methods often leave maximin at $0$, ignoring small infected communities entirely. Two further findings stand out: fairness acts as a \emph{catalyst}, since spreading seeds across communities avoids overlapping influence regions and raises total saved nodes at larger $k$ (the worst-case cost was only ${\sim}2\%$ at $k\le20$); and every method's fairness drops as the number of rumor sources or the edge probability grows, because more communities get infected than the budget can cover.

\subsection{Competing cascades with user bias: CICMB \cite{cicmb}}
Each user $v$ has time-varying biases $B_m(v)$ toward misinformation and $B_t(v)$ toward the truth; an $M$-node converts $v$ with probability $P(u,v)\,B_m(v)$, and a $T$-node with $P(u,v)\,B_t(v)$. Adopting one message halves (or squares) the bias toward the other, modeling confirmation bias. Given a deadline $\alpha$ and a pool $P$ of \emph{willing} campaigners, $k$-TruthScore estimates per-step adoption probabilities on a DAG version of the graph, scores each candidate by the truth probability it delivers to nodes the rumor threatens, and returns the top $k$. It saves ${\sim}30$--$42\%$ of threatened nodes on Digg, Facebook and Twitter, outperforming TIB and TMB. Savings fall as rumor sources increase, peak when the deadline is about the graph's diameter, and are lower under the quadratic bias update, because users harden faster once they believe one side.

\subsection{Fairness objectives \cite{welfare,fang}}
Existing notions each fix one view: \emph{maximin} (raise the worst group), \emph{diversity constraints} (each group does at least as well as with its size-proportional budget used internally), and \emph{demographic parity} (DP; equal rates). Rahmattalabi et al.\ \cite{welfare} instead maximize the isoelastic welfare
\[
W_\alpha(\mathbf u)=\textstyle\sum_c N_c\,u_c^{\alpha}/\alpha\quad(\alpha<1),\qquad W_0=\textstyle\sum_c N_c\log u_c,
\]
where $u_c$ is community $c$'s expected reached fraction. $\alpha=1$ is utilitarian, $\alpha=0$ proportional, and $\alpha\to-\infty$ leximin. $W_\alpha$ satisfies five welfare principles, including a new \emph{influence transfer principle}, and is monotone submodular, so greedy keeps $1-1/e$. No welfare function can always satisfy their sixth principle, \emph{utility gap reduction}, because cross-community edges couple utilities (Prop.~2); it does hold when communities are disconnected (Prop.~3). On SBMs and a 5,940-person landslide-preparedness network in Sitka, Alaska, varying $\alpha$ spans utility gaps of 4--26\% against a price of fairness of 36--5\%, and differences in community \emph{connectedness} move the price of fairness by up to 25\%, versus under 4\% for differences in size.

Fang et al.\ \cite{fang} bring DP to blocking, arguing it fits better than maximin or welfare there: groups start with unequal rumor exposure, and DP asks that each group's share of protection match its share of exposure, so the intervention neither neglects sparse minorities nor widens existing gaps. On Zachary's Karate Club they show that maximin, DC, welfare and DP give visibly different protection splits across three communities. They define a concave DP-aware objective $\mathcal W=\sum_c n_c^{1-\alpha}x_c^{\alpha}$, where $x_c$ is $c$'s share of blocking and $n_c$ its share of exposure, prove it is $(\kappa,\epsilon)$-\emph{approximately} monotone submodular, and show greedy reaches $(1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}-\psi$. Scalarizing with total blocking traces a Pareto front, and their lazy greedy CELF-R tolerates bounded violations of diminishing returns. On networks up to 1.6M nodes, CELF-R's front dominates maximin, DC and welfare baselines and runs 48--60\% faster than full recomputation, while the LP-based DP baseline did not finish within 12 hours.

\section{Relation to course topics}
\textbf{Finding influential sets.} All four papers start from the course's $k$-seed influence maximization problem and its greedy $1-1/e$ guarantee for monotone submodular objectives \cite{kleinberg}. They show what happens to that guarantee once fairness is added. Total blocking under COICM is still submodular, but maximin is not, so FWRRS falls back on a heuristic. Welfare fairness keeps submodularity by applying a concave function to each group's coverage. Fang et al.\ recover an approximate guarantee through approximate submodularity.

\textbf{Probabilistic contagion and threshold models.} Both FWRRS and Fang et al.\ rely on reverse-reachable sampling, which is the standard live-edge view of the IC model \cite{kleinberg} (flip every edge's coin in advance, then check who can reach whom) run backward from a target. CICMB's biases act like personal adoption thresholds whose values change with history, linking IC contagion to the course's threshold models.

\textbf{Network structure.} As in the classic result that dense clusters block cascades \cite{kleinberg}, every paper finds outcomes driven by homophily and community density. Small, tightly knit or sparsely connected groups are the ones fairness-blind seeding neglects, and reaching them is what makes fairness costly. This is interesting because it moves the question from \emph{how far} a cascade spreads to \emph{who} it reaches, and because the papers disagree on whether fairness costs efficiency: Rahmattalabi et al.\ find a price of fairness above 40\% for maximin, while FWRRS finds fairness can increase total blocking.

\textbf{Collective action.} Two competing cascades form a race in which early adopters tip their neighbors; confirmation bias in CICMB is a simple mechanism for echo chambers.

\section{Weaknesses and improvements}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Receptiveness is never group-structured.} FWRRS, Fang et al.\ and Rahmattalabi et al.\ give every group identical propagation probabilities, so groups differ only in position. CICMB varies bias, but draws it independently and uniformly at random per node, unrelated to community or neighbors, whereas real political views are closer to bimodal and clustered. A more realistic model would sample bias by community, e.g.\ from a bimodal mixture with each community centered on one mode, so groups differ on average while individuals still vary.
\item \textbf{Missing fairness or missing receptiveness.} CICMB optimizes only total saved nodes. The fairness papers omit bias. Neither line studies their combination.
\item \textbf{Weak guarantees.} FWRRS's six-step rule has no approximation bound, and k-TruthScore ranks candidates independently, ignoring overlap, and scores truth reach without checking that the truth arrives first. Selecting seeds greedily by marginal gain, and comparing against brute force on small graphs, would show how far these heuristics are from optimal.
\item \textbf{Fragile fairness measurement.} Maximin over thousands of Louvain clusters (2,445 in FWRRS's PHY network) can be pinned at $0$ by one unreachable tiny community, and $W_\alpha$ is $-\infty$ when any $u_c=0$. Fang et al.\ report only their own fairness score, not the worst-off community, and run baselines at a single hyperparameter setting. Softer objectives, a minimum community size, per-community results and tuned baselines would make these comparisons more reliable.
\item \textbf{Results are hard to compare.} Each paper uses its own fairness metric (maximin, equity and disparity, a DP score, utility gap), its own price-of-fairness convention (a ratio vs.\ a percentage loss), its own community definition (Louvain clusters vs.\ demographic groups) and its own diffusion model (IC vs.\ LT). A shared benchmark reporting every metric on the same graphs and communities would let the methods be compared directly.
\item \textbf{Timing and knowledge assumptions.} All papers assume a fully known network. Both campaigns start at once in CICMB, its biases decay toward $0$ over time, and it restricts campaigners to a fixed pool even though, online, bots can be injected on either side at any time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Further research directions}
\textbf{1. Fair blocking with group-dependent acceptance (our proposal).} Add an acceptance rate $a_g$ to COICM: a node in group $g$ adopts the truth with probability $a_g$, and a node that rejects the truth stays inactive and can still get the rumor. Then compare maximin, DP and $\alpha$-welfare seeding. In a simple allocation model where each seed in $g$ saves $b\,a_g$ nodes, maximin needs $k_g\propto n_g/a_g$, giving, with $w_g=n_g/N$ and $r=a_H/a_L$,
\[
\mathrm{PoF}=\frac{\text{best total}}{\text{maximin total}}=w_H+r\,w_L ,
\]
which is linear in the receptiveness gap and unbounded as $a_L\to0$. (Rahmattalabi et al.\ report PoF as the loss $1-1/\mathrm{PoF}$.) Maximizing $W_\alpha$ gives $k_g\propto n_g\,a_g^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$, one dial running from ``all seeds to the receptive group'' ($\alpha\to1$) through size-proportional parity ($\alpha=0$) to maximin ($\alpha\to-\infty$). We plan to test both formulas by simulation on two-group SBMs, sweeping $a_L$ and homophily, with community biases drawn from a bimodal distribution. Our hypothesis is that the real price of fairness will \emph{exceed} the formula, because a resistant group also forwards the truth less, so the effective gap compounds beyond $a_H/a_L$. Rahmattalabi et al.'s finding that structural differences drive PoF far more than group size suggests the same.

\textbf{2. Does submodularity survive?} Unequal acceptance breaks submodularity even for total blocking. Take edges $s$--$x$, $s$--$y$, $x$--$w$, $y$--$w$ (all live), with the rumor at $s$ and $w$ rejecting corrections. Then seed $y$ saves 1 node alone but 2 after $x$, because shielding $w$ requires cutting \emph{every} rumor path, which makes seeds complements. To see how much this matters in practice, we will compare greedy against brute force on Karate Club as $a_L$ falls.

\textbf{3. Strategic and timed campaigns.} Let the rumor source target the least-protected group after seeing the truth seeds (a Stackelberg game), add a deadline, or inject low-influence bot seeds on both sides over time.

\textbf{4. Messengers and edges.} Let acceptance depend on whether sender and receiver share a group, testing in-group messengers, and study budgets on \emph{edges} (rewarding people for forwarding the truth) instead of seed nodes.

\textbf{5. Learning $a_g$.} Estimate acceptance rates from observed fact-check cascades and test how robust fair seed choices are to estimation error.

\end{multicols}
