\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Strict parity lifts the worst group no further than maximin.} At $\lambda=2$, DP greedy's worst rate is within noise of worst-first's ($0.140$, $0.137$), but its total is far lower ($0.149$, $0.271$; PoF $2.47$, $1.35$). The extra cost buys a smaller gap by holding $H$ back. At $k=30$ the worst rates are again close ($0.355$, $0.344$), and worst-first saves $13\%$ more in total.
\item \emph{Looser parity closes most of that difference.} At $\lambda=0.6$, DP greedy matches worst-first's worst rate within noise and has a higher total. $\lambda=0.75$ is within noise of soft-min greedy on total, worst rate and gap, with the same number of seeds in $L$. So ``maximin is much cheaper than parity'' holds for strict $\lambda$, not in general.
\item \emph{Saved leaves little to choose between.} Worst-first keeps all ten seeds in $H$, and its total and worst rate are slightly above total greedy's. Since greedy is a heuristic and this is one set of realizations, we do not read this as fairness raising the total. Strict parity gives up about $0.04$ of total for a smaller gap, with no gain in worst rate.
\item \emph{Worst-first ($7$) and DP greedy at $\lambda\ge0.6$ ($6$ to $10$) place more seeds in $L$ than Claim 1's $4$ of $10$} on the converted outcome, so the ceiling model's shared allocation is only a rough guide.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Open questions.} We compared the looser penalties only at this one setting. We expect loose parity to stay close to maximin at other $\mu_L$ and $k$, including the $k=30$ tie-break effect, but have not tested it. We also have not tested how the comparison changes with many communities: the gap $\Delta_{DP}$ depends only on the best and worst communities, so it may share maximin's fragility (Fig.~\ref{fig:maximin-fragility}).
